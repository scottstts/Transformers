# Rendering and startup

The app uses Three's WebGPU backend and TSL node materials. The renderer's built-in WebGL fallback is disabled immediately after construction, and initialization verifies that a WebGPU device was obtained. Device loss and uncaptured GPU errors retain Three's default logging and also stop the game through the app's error path.

`platform/device.ts` gates startup before the dynamic game import: only desktop Chromium browsers with mouse input proceed. Other browsers and mobile devices stay on the existing entry artwork with a desktop Chromium message; no renderer, assets or game systems start. Startup then remains observed through dynamic module loading, renderer initialization, scene creation, shader compilation, first render, and GPU queue completion. The selected vehicle loader resolves only after both the vehicle and its combat weapon have downloaded and decoded. During shader compilation the hidden combat weapon/effects (and the specials' billows, arcs and the world's debris) are forced through one real draw, including the weapon's cast-shadow path, and the GPU queue is drained before the normal first frame can be declared ready. The entry screen (`index.html`, `platform/boot-ui.ts`) shows boot progress as a count of the seven reported stages (`BOOT_STEPS`); a sun rises over a drawn ridge line and a ruler gauge fills. When the first frame is on the GPU, the gauge opens into the Enter button and the drawing fades to show the live scene behind. The live scene opens on a low broadside of the car from its sunlit side (`FollowCamera.showSide`). The entry type sits in the sky above the car, and the controls legend sits at the bottom edge, so the car stays clear between them. The broadside is only the starting orbit and stays in place after Enter. The first mouse look orbits away from it. Once the car moves, the usual drive follow swings the camera in behind. On narrow desktop windows the broadside backs off until the whole car fits across (`SIDE_HALF_SPAN`). That override ends at the first look or movement, and the distance then eases back to the normal framing. Enter requests pointer lock, and play starts on `pointerlockchange`, so a refused lock leaves the button there for another try. On failure the same screen becomes the diagnostic view (`#boot-error`). Adding or removing a `setBootStage` call means updating `BOOT_STEPS`.

The shader-compile stage (`GameSession.compile`) warms everything the world can show, not just the start view: effects, the soldiers' every tier and their health bars, and the fortress are made visible and frustum culling is switched off for one `compileAsync` and one real hidden draw (`rendering/warm.ts`). Fortress detail has its own distance visibility rule, so `Forts.showAllDetail` temporarily exposes those meshes too. The earlier culling override alone left them hidden until the car approached a district. The normal visibility state is restored before play. A first car switch likewise draws the new character under the switch cover: for its settle frames the session forces its combat effects' warm state and every hidden object under its effects (`revealHidden`) on just before each draw, after the frame's update has hidden idle ones again. The draw must come from the animation loop: three draws a scene pass once per frame, so a `pipeline.render()` issued from the switch itself reused the pass already drawn that frame and never drew the Semi's flashes, tracers and casings, which then built at its first burst.

The scene's point lights are a fixed set of slots (`rendering/light-slots.ts`, three: the most any car carries), added before the warm-up. three keys every lit material's shader on the ids of the scene's lights, so a switch that brought the new car's own `PointLight` objects invalidated every lit shader in the world; each rebuilt the next time it was drawn (fort buildings and sand drifts driving out of the fort, ~37 shaders). A car's lights are taken out of its effects when the session builds it (`LightSlots.adopt`) and are copied onto the slots each frame, so they must be positioned in world space under untransformed parents (adopt refuses otherwise). Never add a light to the scene during play; give a new effect light to its character (within the slot count) instead. `node tools/switch-probe.mjs [from] [to]` replays a switch headlessly and counts the pipelines built afterwards in play (should be 0).


While a car switch loads (`switchCharacter`), the world's clock is stopped: the fight, the soldiers and their debris stand still until the new car is in, so nothing happens behind the cover that the player could not answer.

`#app` is `position: fixed; inset: 0`: the canvas always covers the viewport, and the page background never shows beneath it, even if the document scrolls or resizes oddly.

Resizes commit once per animation frame through `setDrawingBufferSize`. The base DPR is capped at 1.7 and at 4,000,000 drawing-buffer pixels, including when the resulting DPR is below 1. The scene uses ACES tone mapping and a node bloom pass. Its bright pass is a soft knee (`look.ts`): what a pixel carries above the threshold, eased in over 0.5. The stock pass switched a pixel's whole radiance on at the threshold, so the dusty sky round the sun bloomed with a hard edge along its threshold contour and veiled the car in front of it. Strong highlights bloom almost as before; lamps a little less. The post pass reads the scene through the heat shimmer (`rendering/heat-shimmer.ts`: over sun-baked sand, rays that run long and low waver, weighted by the pixel's distance and by how near the horizon it looks; one depth read and one noise fetch, no extra pass) and through the lens reactions of a special (`rendering/lens.ts`: a blast wave's refraction ring, an exposure flash before tone mapping, a drained grade; specials.md); at rest they are exactly neutral. The film grade (`rendering/grade.ts`) runs after tone mapping and output encoding, so it works on display values. The pipeline therefore turns off `outputColorTransform` and calls `renderOutput` itself before the grade. The grade applies a blend toward a smoothstep S-curve for contrast, cool shadows and warm highlights, an elliptical vignette and per-frame hashed grain that is strongest in the mid-tones. It uses arithmetic only, with no extra passes or texture reads. The grain also hides banding in the sky gradient.

## Sun shadows and baked light

- `SunShadowNode` (`rendering/sun-shadow.ts`) is the sun's shadow node. It samples the cascades (moving casters, every frame) and four cached static levels (the fortress), and takes the darker. The levels are light-space rectangles round the camera (24, 110, 190 and 720 m half-width at 2048, 4096, 4096 and 2048 texels). Texels of 5 cm reach ~90 m, so a watchtower's 11 cm bracing still shadows a wall across a yard; at 12 cm texels it dropped out beyond ~60 m. Each level stores 16-bit depth (steps under 3 cm over its depth window, far inside the bias) and an 8-bit colour buffer, which a shadow pass never uses but a render target must have: 3 bytes a texel instead of three's default 8, ~90 MB for all four. They are foreshortened along the sun's azimuth, since a low sun needs less height than width. Each is snapped to its own texel grid and redrawn only when the camera moves its snap step (a tenth of the level). The finest level may redraw whenever it must; coarser levels share a budget of one per frame, and all draw on the first frame. Along the sun each level's depth window is its half-width plus 60 m either side of its centre. Its receivers lie up to the half-width off the camera plus the camera's height, and the centre's depth snaps in half-width steps. A window of only the half-width left the far side of the finest levels unshadowed, and those shadows popped as the camera moved. They cross-fade finest to coarsest in uniform control flow (every level sampled every time), with normal bias scaled by texel size and filter radius by its inverse. The penumbra is the same width in metres at every level. Filtering a coarse level over as many texels as the finest smeared thin casters (a watchtower's lattice) to nothing beyond ~50 m. Below a one-texel radius a thin caster's shadow is lost to resolution rather than blur. A level's pass draws only the static casters, which keep `castShadow` off for the cascades. The far level shadows the fortress from the start, a few hundred metres off.
- A level with no static caster in it moves without a redraw (its map stays clear), so out in the open desert the levels cost nothing. Before, the finest one redrew an empty map every 2.4 m of travel.
- The warm-up (`GameSession.compile`) reveals the whole world (`DesertWorld.reveal`): the fortress's distance-hidden detail and at least one instance in each terrain patch draw. The first frame draws every static shadow level with culling off, so every fort mesh is built into every level pass before play. Afterwards all levels are redrawn, because the warm-up drew hidden detail into them. Anything first drawn mid-game builds its node material then, a hitch of up to ~0.2 s. That is why the dust devils are never hidden, only faded.
- A headless check of the levels must advance three's frame (`renderer._nodes.nodeFrame.update()`, which the animation loop does) before each render. Otherwise the scene pass, a once-per-frame node, never re-renders, and the levels appear frozen.
- The fortress's ambient occlusion is a one-time compute bake after renderer init (`DesertWorld.prepare`). The session and every preview tool call it after `bakeEnvironment`; until it runs, the map reads as open sky.
- The environment bake is the atmosphere's sky over the sunlit sand's radiance, at intensity 1, with no hemisphere light beside it.
